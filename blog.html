About SPARC

St. Xavier’s College, Kathmandu, is an educational institution established and managed by the Nepal Jesuit Society. The college is dedicated to excellence and service, upholding the motto: “Live for God and Lead for Nepal.”

St. Xavier’s Prototyping, Automation and Robotics Club (SPARC) is the pioneering robotics club run by the A-Levels department. Our establishment serves the purpose of bringing student ideas into innovative projects, while providing a platform where they can present and compete among peers.

Over the past eight months, our members have participated in 10+ events and emerged victorious in several. Notable achievements include third place in an IEEE competition, second place in the Orbit International Engineering Expo, and participation in the Technoxian World Robotics Competition in India.

Unlike conventional robotics clubs in Kathmandu, SPARC focuses on developing ideas that aim to create real-world impact, seen through the perspectives of teenagers.

Mission Statement

Our club's mission is to establish a tradition for a hub where studentship meets purpose.

About Our Major Event Organized in 2025: SPARC FEST

SPARC FEST is a one-of-a-kind hardware hackathon striving for innovation in the themes of disaster management, assisting the disabled, environmental sustainability, urban waste management, and healthcare.

High school is where students explore their interests in depth, and we believe that a platform for future innovators to learn and compete is currently lacking. SPARC FEST 2025 promises to be an impactful gathering for creative minds to come together.

The cosmos is the entirety of existence—everything that is, was, and ever will be. Its tales are reflections of our own story. It is a saga of how wandering bands of hunters and gatherers reached for the stars and beyond, a single narrative shaped by many heroes. The scale and age of the Cosmos defy ordinary human comprehension, placing our small, fragile home, Earth, amidst its boundless expanse.

Throughout human history, humanity has evolved to imagine, wonder, and, most importantly, recognize that understanding is key to survival. The cosmos is rich beyond measure, and our existence within it is akin to a mote of dust drifting through the morning sky.

Our most fleeting reflections on the cosmos evoke a deep, almost visceral response—a shiver along the spine, a catch in the voice, a sensation like a distant memory of falling from a great height. Yet, as we explore these mysteries, we are drawn ever closer to the ultimate enigma.

Drawing from the insights of astronomer Carl Sagan, the preceding piece defines our existence within the cosmos. From the humblest of beginnings, humanity has gazed upward, contemplating the boundless possibilities offered by the infinite sky. We have long questioned our origin and purpose.

Historically, people believed Earth was the centre of the universe. However, through centuries of discovery and the power of imagination, we have come to realise that Earth is merely a speck in the vast cosmos.

To grasp this scale, consider a grain of sand: on the cosmic scale, that tiny area covered by the sand contains nearly 10,000 galaxies and over a quadrillion stars. There are more planets in the universe than there are grains of sand on Earth.

Less than a century ago, we were unaware of any universe beyond our own Milky Way Galaxy. Our perspective and understanding of the universe have expanded enormously since then.

Amid the vastness of the universe, where does our tiny blue planet fit in? The first line of our address begins here, on Earth. Everything and everyone we know has drawn breath on this planet, within a time frame that seems insignificant compared to the age of the universe.

Leaving Earth, our nearest celestial neighbour, the Moon, has no sky, no ocean, and no life—scarred by the impacts of cosmic events. Our beacon of light at night, the Sun, holds all the planets in our Solar System with its gravitational pull, from Mercury to the outermost planets, Uranus and Neptune.

Beyond these planets lie thousands of frozen worlds, as well as billions of comets in the Oort Cloud, remnants from the formation of the solar system. This enormous cloud of comets encloses our solar system, marking the second line of our cosmic address.

Next, we locate our solar system within the Milky Way Galaxy, about thirty thousand light-years from its centre. The galaxy next door is the great spiralling Andromeda Galaxy.

Our two giant galaxies, along with a few smaller ones, form the Local Group, our next line of address. Our galaxy is just one of thousands in the Virgo Supercluster, each containing billions of stars and countless worlds.

Yet, the Virgo Supercluster is merely a small part of the universe, leading us to the final line of our cosmic address: the Observable Universe.

In considering our place in the cosmos, we encounter a profound philosophical and scientific debate: the tension between randomness and determinism.

Determinism posits that occurrences in nature are determined by preceding events or natural laws, suggesting that everything leading up to this point has happened for a reason and that everything has a purpose and order.

In contrast, the perspective of randomness argues that events are mere coincidences. According to this view, our existence is the result of a complex but inevitable series of chemical accidents and biological mutations, with no grand meaning or purpose.

This ongoing debate not only challenges our understanding of existence but also influences how we interpret our place within the vast universe.

From a cosmic perspective, most human concerns may seem insignificant. However, we must also remember that a living cell is as complex and beautiful as the realms of galaxies and stars.

Life is perhaps the greatest gift the cosmos has bestowed upon us. Each of us is shaped by our experiences, and the essence of life lies in the power of our thoughts and the value of our memories.

Our perspectives on freedom, pleasure, prosperity, and duty define our character. Ultimately, it is not from the vastness of space that we must seek meaning, but from the governance of our own thoughts.

My Experience of Organizing SPARC FEST 2025

I led the SPARC Robotics Club, which provided me with the opportunity to hone my leadership skills and pursue my passion for robotics. Because things did not always go according to plan, SPARC became the place where I learned the most. Being in a position where I could significantly impact the club’s trajectory broadened my planning and analytical skills.

On 27th July, we organized SPARC FEST 2025, Nepal’s first robotics hackathon. The process began two months earlier. Coming to college almost every day even after finishing our board exams, often after staying awake until midnight the previous night, was exhausting. At times, the three of us could barely manage it.

Drafting proposals for sponsors and approaching them felt pointless at one stage due to the low success rate. We heard the same response repeatedly: “It’s the end of the fiscal year, and we don’t have any budget.” In total, we approached nearly 50 companies.

Eventually, things began to fall into place. Securing our title sponsor just a week and a half before the event gave us enough time to organize finances and set our plans in motion. That week was overwhelmingly hectic, but I clearly remember the sense of relief when all participating teams were confirmed and our budget of NPR 1.5 lakhs proved sufficient.

On the day of the event, we were prepared for far worse, but to our surprise, everything ran smoothly. By 6 p.m., the event had concluded, the venue was cleaned, and we headed home. Receiving congratulations from robotics teachers, engineers, and business professionals was deeply reassuring.

Looking back, the experience was entirely worth it. I learned that even the smallest events require meticulous planning, and challenges that appear minor often demand significant effort to resolve.

Most importantly, this experience taught me that if I truly want to achieve something, I can make time for it. Leading SPARC significantly increased the time I dedicated to activities that strengthened my abilities and helped me recognize my potential.

Every day, as I return home from school on public transport, I see a blind man navigating his way home without assistance. With no tactile pavements or traffic signals, every step is a shot in the dark—yet he never falters. How many times must he have stumbled to memorize every bump in the road?

The Palm Pilot is an all-in-one tool intended to change the lives of disabled people. Featuring many capabilities that are constantly improving, it aims to become an everyday-use item for many people in our society.

Objectives

To help disabled people in their daily lives.

To monitor patients in a hospital environment.

To allow easy supervision of patients by guardians.

To minimise risk for disabled people.

Method, Observation and Application

The Palm Pilot is a wearable glove designed to assist disabled individuals in their daily lives. Its features include monitoring location, continuous monitoring of hand movements and heart rate updated periodically to a website, the ability to control household appliances through hand gestures, and the ability to control a wheelchair.

It can also be used in hospital wards, allowing nurses to monitor multiple patients via a web interface. The glove provides basic entertainment by enabling wireless control of games on a computer screen or an external display. It can also control a laptop cursor and perform actions such as opening a YouTube video in a browser.

Motion and touch sensors are used to capture hand movement. Radio communication is used for some features, while internet connectivity is used for others. The data collected is updated to a database and used in real time by multiple programs.

Technologies Used

Arduino with ESP32 using C++ and C#.

Database for storing real-time hand motion data.

Python for functionalities including games and cursor movement.

JavaScript with React for the website.

Conclusion

The Palm Pilot is a versatile project with multiple real-world use cases. It is intended to develop into a future product after further stages of refinement.

Designing the Palm Pilot was not only an exercise in engineering, but also an effort to address real challenges and uphold social responsibility.

“Intelligence is the ability to adapt to change.”

Stephen Hawking was 21 years old when he became physically restricted, but his brilliant mind knew no boundaries. The story about black holes formed in laboratories with science seemingly unimaginable begins with the brilliancy of Stephen Hawking and his breakthrough. The 1974 paper by Hawking titled “Black Hole Explosions?” and a follow-up paper in 1975 attempted a union of quantum mechanics and general relativity to show that black holes should, in theory, leak their mass in the form of what we now know as Hawking radiation.

But first, what is a black hole? A black hole is an area of such immense gravity that nothing can escape from it, not even light. The event horizon of the black hole is the point beyond which the velocity required to escape the black hole exceeds the speed of light. Before diving into the world of leaking black holes, there is a need to first understand some principles of quantum mechanics. This is where we let go of the limitations of the physical world we know and explore what can't be seen.

Quantum physics is the science of the unseen world. It studies matter and energy at the most fundamental level. To go deeper into the quantum world, imagine empty space in the universe. Doesn’t really seem like a step ahead, does it? In the quantum world, space isn’t empty. It is filled with fields that vibrate at different frequencies, much like the strings on a guitar. These frequencies can be positive or negative. When a field is in a vacuum state, there’s a balance between the positive and negative frequencies, which can crudely be thought of as a balance of virtual matter and antimatter particles. These various positive and negative frequency modes virtually annihilate or cancel out so that no real particles exist. Crudely, this can be known as quantum field theory.

Hawking Radiation is more than just radiation from a black hole. To understand this, imagine a trajectory of a quantum field in vacuum extending from the infinite past to the infinite future. A giant star collapses in the trajectory and forms a black hole. Just as the black hole is created, it wreaks havoc on the quantum field. A field that was in a perfectly vacuum state before the creation of the black hole is now disturbed, causing a disturbance to the fundamental vibrational mode of the positive and negative frequencies that define the vacuum. By the time the trajectory finds its way back out into flat space, it seems like the black hole is leaking particles. Radiation is seen emerging from the black hole to distant observers. Since energy can neither be created nor destroyed, the particle cannot just exist out of thin air. So, the energy required for the particle to exist is drawn from the disruptor itself, the black hole. Crudely, this is Hawking radiation as defined in Stephen Hawking’s mathematics.

"We know nothing of what will happen in the future, but by the analogy of experience." Analog black holes are made with analogies of real black holes, hence the name ‘analog.’ They are physical systems that aren't actually black holes but behave in similar ways. The story of analog black holes begins with physicist Bill Unruh, who was the first to develop an analogy for laboratory-created black holes in 1972, starting with a pure thought experiment. Unruh imagined a river with a fish constantly producing sound. In still water, the sound that the fish produces is heard in all directions. He pictured that the river leads to a waterfall, and the speed of the water increases to the speed of sound as it approaches the waterfall. As the fish is pushed down the river stream, the sound that it produces will get lesser and lesser until, at a point, the sound won’t be heard. This point will be when the speed of the water in the river is equal to the speed of sound, as the water will push all the sound waves downstream. This is the event horizon of the analog black hole imagination. As the fish gets pushed down the waterfall, it can be compared to getting deeper into the black hole. Like the analogy devised by Unruh, other analogies have been developed but with the same basic principle. Hawking radiation is a quantum mechanical effect, so the model discussed forth will deal with a quantum-level analogy. Jeff Steinhauer and his team have been able to achieve a correlation between Hawking radiation particles and partner particles inside the black hole, along with entanglement of their thermal energies.

Steinhauer’s analogy uses about 8,000 atoms of rubidium in a Bose-Einstein condensate confined in a narrow tube created by a laser beam. This is the medium, like the water in Unruh’s analogy. Bose-Einstein condensate is a state of matter achieved when atoms are cooled to near absolute zero temperature (zero Kelvin). Many lasers are used to create a flow in the condensate, which flows from left to right in the narrow tube. This sonic horizon is the point at which the flow of the condensate changes from subsonic to supersonic speeds. It is flowing slower than the speed of sound before it reaches the sonic horizon, whereas it is flowing faster than the speed of sound after. The flow of the condensate causes phonons to be produced. The phonon is a sound wave created by the vibration of the atomic lattice, here the atomic lattice of the rubidium atoms. Phonon sound waves trying to travel from right to left in the supersonic region cannot go forward because the flow is faster than the speed of sound. It is trapped inside the sonic black hole. Waves in the subsonic region can go forward because the flow of the condensate is slower than the speed of sound. This implies that particles before the sonic horizon or in a real black hole before the event horizon can escape. The wave travelling against the flow of the condensate in the supersonic region experiences a big change in frequency due to the Doppler effect, so big that its frequency becomes negative. The energy it carries also becomes negative since energy is directly proportional to frequency. Whereas in the subsonic region with the slower flow of the condensate, the energy is positive. There can exist pairs, one in the subsonic region and one in the supersonic region, with zero total energy. The part of the pair that escapes the sonic horizon is Hawking radiation in Steinhauer’s analog black hole.

A correlation function describes how microscopic variables, such as spin and density, at different positions are related. The team considered the correlation between two points, x and x’, along the black hole. Point x is outside the black hole, and point x’ is inside the black hole. The outlined quadrant has x values that are negative and x’ values that are positive. The lines are points that mark equal times from the horizon. When a particle moves some distance away and some distance toward, if they move equal times, there are correlations at those points. Repeating the experiment thousands of times results in data regarding the nature of Hawking radiation. A deeper band of points means that there was more correlation, which means there was more Hawking radiation to cause these correlations. The depth of these bands can be ascribed to a temperature. From this, we can extract their respective temperatures from the quantity of Hawking radiation. The temperature can also be extracted from the width of the band. Narrower width means shorter wavelength and higher temperature. The results obtained from both methods roughly coincide with each other, revealing the thermal nature of Hawking radiation and proving Hawking’s calculations. The results are in agreement with numerical simulations and predictions found in the literature, providing both literary and numerical proof. It is one of the very few systems to confirm Hawking’s calculations quantitatively.

Researching black holes and Hawking radiation fulfils a greater purpose than just understanding these mysterious astrophysical entities. To grasp what Hawking radiation truly is, Stephen Hawking needed an amalgamation of quantum mechanics and general relativity—a theory of everything. But a theory of everything did not exist then and does not exist now. Hawking provided a clever workaround to construct a difficult marriage between quantum mechanics and general relativity. His theory shows how such indifferent entities in physics could work together. Another reason for studying Hawking radiation is quantum entanglement, the study of particles where knowledge about one particle immediately reveals knowledge about another particle, or the interrelation between particles that enter and escape a black hole. Analog black holes aim to find the true nature of Hawking radiation to reveal the relation and nature of particles within an actual black hole. Revealing the true nature of Hawking radiation would help devise a theory of everything.

Songs of protest, melodies of hope and harmonies of resilience have defined countless generations. From the chants of ancient tribes to the digital beats of pop, music captures the essence of contemporary society. Music resonates deeply with listeners, amplifying feelings and provoking emotions to be revealed. Music consistently mirrors society's struggles, hopes, and transformations, fosters dialogue about our identity and community, and offers a unique lens through which we can understand and appreciate the complexities of our world. As such, the extent to which music reflects society is profound, serving as a testament to its enduring power to inspire and unite.

Music provides commentary on the artist's experiences which are influenced by their society. It captures the present's struggles and hopes. The deeper meaning behind works of art can be interpreted as shared feelings within society. The song 'Glory' by John Legend and Common along with Italian folk song 'Bella Ciao' have gained prominence as symbols of endurance and resistance. 'Glory' captures the enduring fight for civil rights and racial equality. Similarly, originally sung by the Italian anti-fascist resistance movement, "Bella Ciao" evolved into a protest anthem against oppression and tyranny. This shows that music is a form of free expression and a key to understanding contemporary feelings such as war or inequality present in society.

Music plays a vital role in defining and giving identity to communities within a society. It resonates deeply with individuals, illuminates their cultural and moral differences, and helps in celebrating them. The song "Black or White" by Michael Jackson addresses themes of racial equality and the importance of looking beyond appearances to appreciate our common humanity. Similarly, Lady Gaga's "Born This Way" promotes embracing one's true self regardless of race, religion, or sexual orientation. These songs provide a bridge for accepting debated social issues, showing how music serves as a universal language for acceptance and authenticity.

Music empowers us to visualize the world through a distinct perspective than reality. It makes us realize important issues and enables us to become dreamers for a better world. The song "Imagine" by John Lennon has become an anthem for peace and unity, while "Blowin' in the Wind" by Bob Dylan advocates for broader social activism. Both contemplate the fundamental challenges and aspirations of humanity. This illustrates music's capacity to offer profound insights into the complexities of our world and express society's collective dreams.

While it is often argued that music reflects society, this perspective can overlook how music may be shaped by individual artists. It can sometimes promote negative or harmful messages. However, in a free society, the right to varied expression brings challenging arguments. Imposing censorship and age restrictions can minimize harmful impact. For example, Lana del Rey's "Born to Die" was censored by MTV to limit exposure to younger audiences. This shows that solutions exist and that unhealthy debate should be replaced with healthy discussion.

To sum up, music is a way of presenting and preserving cherished beliefs, values, feelings, and emotions of contemporary society. As Victor Hugo stated, "Music expresses that which cannot be put into words and that which cannot remain silent." Music gives voice to struggles, hopes, and transformations while expressing visions of a better world. It remains a true reflection of society, echoing its differences, challenges, and victories through melodies that speak directly to the human soul.

Stories of hope, words of resilience, and poems of pain—literature reflects the writer's feelings. It has the ability to shape emotions and, at times, make people connect more deeply with their own. Nepal, a country with some of the most underrated yet powerful literature, is a strong example of how writing can influence the way people feel.

In the middle of social conflict and ongoing issues, literature often has a positive impact on people’s emotions. It gives hope by focusing on parts of life that are hard to notice during difficult times. The Nepali novel Palpasa Café by Narayan Wagle captures this well.

Set during the Maoist insurgency, the story follows a young man trying to find love and a sense of identity while the country around him is consumed by war. At a time when personal feelings are pushed aside by larger political struggles, the novel shows that love and understanding still find a way.

Because of this, it leaves readers hoping for a better life, both for the characters and for themselves. Literature also helps people understand their own emotions better.

Some argue that literature only romanticizes emotions and ignores the harsh reality of life by exaggerating feelings through fiction. Parijat’s Shiris Ko Phool is often mentioned in this argument, as it presents a love story during the difficult period of the Maoist insurgency.

However, this view misses the point. Shiris Ko Phool was so impactful that it remains one of the most important works in Nepali literature. For many readers, it offered warmth and comfort at a time when hope was scarce.

In the end, literature does not escape reality—it helps people face it. By expressing pain, hope, and resilience, it allows readers to reflect on their own lives and emotions.

Especially in times of conflict, literature becomes more than just stories; it becomes a way for people to feel understood and less alone.

Visit Here

Districts of Nepal is a web-based project that I built using React.js. It focuses on presenting Nepal’s districts in an interactive way and encouraging people to learn more about my nation.

The goal of this project was to share knowledge about Nepal’s geography in a fun and engaging way by actively challenging users.

The website helps users become familiar with the 77 district names and their organization within the country’s federal structure. By leveraging React’s component-based architecture, the project efficiently manages state and dynamic rendering, ensuring smooth interaction and a responsive user experience.

A 10-minute timer is displayed in the top-right corner of the screen, and districts turn green after they are correctly named. Users can try naming districts such as Kathmandu or Mustang to see the interaction in action.

At the end of the 10 minutes, any districts left unnamed turn red, and the screen displays the names of all districts that were named and missed. Rather than overwhelming users with excessive information, the project focuses on a simple naming game.

Building this site strengthened my understanding of React fundamentals such as reusable components, state management, and dynamically rendering structured data.

Districts of Nepal reflects my interest in using technology to create tools that help people in meaningful ways and serves as a foundation for future enhancements such as maps, quizzes, or deeper administrative data visualization.
